# Voice: English edition

Readers are curious, intelligent and not economists. They know what a government bond is; they don't know what a spread, a basis point or TPI is. Write for them at the standard of a good central-bank explainer.

**The answer first.** The title states the finding. The dek adds who and how. Each section's first sentence is that section's point.

**Plain, exact, unhurried.**
- One idea per paragraph, at most four sentences.
- Sentences of 15–20 words on average, never more than 40.
- Active voice, concrete subjects: "Investors demanded more to hold French bonds", not "A widening was observed".
- Define a technical term the first time it appears, in the sentence or with a glossary mark: `((spread))`.
- Say what a number means: "97bp, the widest since July 2012", not just "97bp".
- "We" is the editor; "the authors" or the institution is the paper. Our opinion goes only in the Insight.

**Headlines** (Glacier): state the finding, at most 90 characters, sentence case, no question, no colon subtitle.

**Claims.**
- Causal words ("caused", "drove", "because") only where the cited paper identifies causality. Otherwise use "went together with", "is associated with", "the authors find that …".
- No forecasts. Write conditions and scenarios: "If X and Y, spreads would …; nothing in the data shows that yet."
- Disagreement is news: when studies differ, say so and say why.
- Every finding sentence carries a citation mark; every number is a fact token (`numbers.md`).

**Mechanics** (Glacier): sentence case everywhere; italics for emphasis (`*word*`), never bold; no exclamation marks, no emoji, no hype ("unprecedented", "shocking", "groundbreaking"); true minus "−" (the token renderer handles numbers); en dash for ranges (2010–12); dates as "14 Oct 2026" or "September 2026".

**Headings** for the explainer: short noun phrases or plain statements: "Where things stand", "Then and now", "What turned 2010–12 into a crisis", "How stress could travel", "What to watch".
